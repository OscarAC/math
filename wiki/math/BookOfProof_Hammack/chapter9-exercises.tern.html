If a statement is true, prove it. If a statement is false, disprove it. These exercises are cumulative, covering all topics addressed in Chapters 1-9.** :::exercise[9.2]{#ex-9-2} For every natural number $n$, the integer $2n^2-4n+31$ is prime. ::: :::answer :::disproof The statement is false. For a counterexample, let $n = 31$. Then $2n^2-4n+31 = 2(31^2)-4(31)+31 = 1829 = 31 \cdot 59$, which is not prime. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[9.4]{#ex-9-4} For every natural number $n$, the integer $n^2 + 17n+17$ is prime. ::: :::answer :::disproof The statement is false. For a counterexample, let $n = 1$. Then $n^2+17n+17 = 1^2 + 17(1) + 17 = 35 = 7 \cdot 5$, which is not prime. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[9.6]{#ex-9-6} If $A,B,C$ and $D$ are sets, then $(A \times B) \cap (C \times D) = (A \cap C) \times (B \cap D)$. ::: :::answer :::proof Observe the following sequence of equalities. $$ \begin{align*} \MoveEqLeft (A \times B) \cap (C \times D)\\ & = \set{(x,y) : (x,y) \in A \times B \land (x,y) \in C \times D} && \text{(definition of $\cap$)}\\ & = \set{(x,y) : (x \in A \land y \in B) \land (x \in C \land y \in D)} && \text{(definition of $\times$)}\\ & = \set{(x,y) : (x \in A \land x \in C) \land (y \in B \land y \in D)} && \text{(regrouping)}\\ & = \set{(x,y) : x \in A \cap C \land y \in B \cap D} && \text{(definition of $\cap$)}\\ & = \set{(x,y) : (x,y) \in (A \cap C) \times (B \cap D)} && \text{(definition of $\times$)}\\ & = (A \cap C) \times (B \cap D) && \text{(definition of set-builder)} \end{align*} $$ ::: :::/answer :::exercise[9.8]{#ex-9-8} If $A,B$ and $C$ are sets, then $A - (B \cup C) = (A - B) \cup (A - C)$. ::: :::answer :::disproof The statement is false. For a counterexample, let $A = \set{1,2}$, $B = \set{1}$ and $C = \set{2}$. Then $A - (B \cup C) = \set{1,2} - \set{1,2} = \emptyset$, but $(A - B) \cup (A - C) = \set{2} \cup \set{1} = \set{1,2}$. Since $\emptyset \ne \set{1,2}$, the two sides are not equal. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[9.10]{#ex-9-10} If $A$ and $B$ are sets and $A \cap B = \emptyset$, then $\mathcal{P}(A) -\mathcal{P}(B) \subseteq \mathcal{P}(A - B)$. ::: :::answer :::proof Suppose $A \cap B = \emptyset$. Let $X \in \mathcal{P}(A) - \mathcal{P}(B)$. Then $X \in \mathcal{P}(A)$ and $X \notin \mathcal{P}(B)$, so $X \subseteq A$. Now we claim $A - B = A$. Let $a \in A - B$. Then $a \in A$ and $a \notin B$. Thus every element of $A - B$ is an element of $A$, so $A - B \subseteq A$. Conversely, let $a \in A$. If $a \in B$, then $a \in A$ and $a \in B$, so $a \in A \cap B = \emptyset$, which is impossible. Thus $a \notin B$. So we have $a \in A$ and $a \notin B$, which means $a \in A - B$. Hence $A \subseteq A - B$. Therefore $A - B = A$. So we have $X \subseteq A = A - B$, thus $X \in \mathcal{P}(A-B)$. Since $X$ was arbitrary, $\mathcal{P}(A) - \mathcal{P}(B) \subseteq \mathcal{P}(A-B)$. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[9.12]{#ex-9-12} If $a,b,c \in \mathbb{N}$ and $ab, bc$ and $ac$ all have the same parity, then $a,b$ and $c$ all have the same parity. ::: :::answer :::disproof The statement is false. For a counterexample, let $a = 2$, $b = 1$ and $c = 2$. Then $ab = 2$, $bc = 2$ and $ac = 4$ are all even, so $ab$, $bc$ and $ac$ have the same parity. But $a$ and $c$ are even while $b$ is odd, so $a$, $b$ and $c$ do not all have the same parity. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[9.14]{#ex-9-14} If $A$ and $B$ are sets, then $\mathcal{P}(A) \cap \mathcal{P}(B) = \mathcal{P}(A \cap B)$. ::: :::answer :::proof First we prove $\mathcal{P}(A) \cap \mathcal{P}(B) \subseteq \mathcal{P}(A \cap B)$. Suppose $X \in \mathcal{P}(A) \cap \mathcal{P}(B)$. By definition of intersection, this means $X \in \mathcal{P}(A)$ and $X \in \mathcal{P}(B)$. By definition of power set, $X \subseteq A$ and $X \subseteq B$. Since $X \subseteq A$ and $X \subseteq B$, every element of $X$ lies in both $A$ and $B$, hence in $A \cap B$; therefore $X \subseteq A \cap B$. By definition of power set, this means $X \in \mathcal{P}(A \cap B)$. Since $X$ was arbitrary, $\mathcal{P}(A) \cap \mathcal{P}(B) \subseteq \mathcal{P}(A \cap B)$. Now we show $\mathcal{P}(A \cap B) \subseteq \mathcal{P}(A) \cap \mathcal{P}(B)$. Suppose $X \in \mathcal{P}(A \cap B)$. By definition of power set, this means $X \subseteq A \cap B$; in other words, every element of $X$ lies in both $A$ and $B$. Thus $X \subseteq A$ and $X \subseteq B$. Hence, by definition of power set, $X \in \mathcal{P}(A)$ and $X \in \mathcal{P}(B)$. Thus, by definition of intersection, $X \in \mathcal{P}(A) \cap \mathcal{P}(B)$. Since $X$ was arbitrary, $\mathcal{P}(A \cap B) \subseteq \mathcal{P}(A) \cap \mathcal{P}(B)$. Therefore, since $\mathcal{P}(A) \cap \mathcal{P}(B) \subseteq \mathcal{P}(A \cap B)$ and $\mathcal{P}(A \cap B) \subseteq \mathcal{P}(A) \cap \mathcal{P}(B)$, it follows that $\mathcal{P}(A) \cap \mathcal{P}(B) = \mathcal{P}(A \cap B)$. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[9.16]{#ex-9-16} If $A$ and $B$ are finite sets, then $|A \cup B| = |A| + |B|$. ::: :::answer :::disproof The statement is false. For a counterexample, let $A = \set{1,2}$ and $B = \set{1}$. Then $|A \cup B| = |\set{1,2}| = 2$, and $|A| + |B| = 2 + 1 = 3$. Since $2 \neq 3$, we have $|A \cup B| \neq |A| + |B|$. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[9.18]{#ex-9-18} If $a,b,c \in \mathbb{N}$, then at least one of $a-b$, $a+c$, and $b-c$ is even. ::: :::answer :::proof Assume $a,b,c \in \mathbb{N}$. Then each is either even or odd. By the pigeonhole principle, at least two must have the same parity. If $a$ and $b$ have the same parity, then $a-b$ is even. If $a$ and $c$ have the same parity, then $a+c$ is even. If $b$ and $c$ have the same parity, then $b-c$ is even. Therefore at least one of $a-b$,$a+c$ and $b-c$ is even. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[9.20]{#ex-9-20} There exist prime numbers $p$ and $q$ for which $p-q=1000$. ::: :::answer :::proof Let $p$ be the prime number 2009 and let $q$ be the prime number 1009. Then $p-q = 2009-1009 = 1000$. ::: :::/answer
