Since $x = x \cdot 1$ and $1 \in \mathbb{Z}$, the definition of divisibility gives $x \mid x$. Thus $\mid$ is reflexive. Next, we will show that $\mid$ is transitive. For this, we must show that if $x \mid y$ and $y \mid z$ then $x \mid z$. Suppose $x \mid y$ and $y \mid z$. This means $y = xm$ and $z = yn$ for some integers $m$ and $n$. Substituting $xm$ for $y$ we get $z = (xm)n = x(mn)$, and by definition of divisibility, we see $x \mid z$. The past two paragraphs show that $\mid$ is reflexive and transitive on $\mathbb{Z}$. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[11.1.14]{#ex-11-1-14} Suppose $R$ is a symmetric and transitive relation on a set $A$, and there is an element $a \in A$ for which $aRx$ for every $x \in A$. Prove that $R$ is reflexive. ::: :::answer :::proof Let $R$ be a transitive and symmetric relation in $A$. And assume there is an element $a \in A$ such that $aRx$ for every $x \in A$. We want to prove that $xRx$ for every $x \in A$. Take any $x \in A$. Since $aRx$ for every $x \in A$, we have $aRx$. Because $R$ is symmetric, we have $xRa$. So we have both $xRa$ and $aRx$. Because $R$ is transitive, $xRa$ and $aRx$ imply $xRx$. Therefore, $R$ is transitive, symmetric, and reflexive. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[11.1.16]{#ex-11-1-16} Define a relation $R$ on $\mathbb{Z}$ by declaring that $xRy$ if and only if $x^2 \equiv y^2 \pmod{4}$. Prove that $R$ is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. ::: :::answer :::proof Assume $R$ is a relation on $\mathbb{Z}$ such as $xRy$ if and only if $x^2 \equiv y^2 \pmod{4}$. We want to prove that $R$ is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. First, we will show that $R$ is reflexive. Take any integer $x \in Z$, and observe that $4 \mid 0$, so $4 \mid x^2 - x^2$. Thus, by definition, $x^2 \equiv x^2 \pmod{4}$. This shows $R$ is reflexive. Second, we will show that $R$ is symmetric. Suppose $xRy$ for some $x,y \in \mathbb{Z}$. So $4 \mid (x^2 - y^2)$. This means $(x^2 - y^2) = 4m$ for some $m \in \mathbb{Z}$. Then $y^2 - x^2 = 4(-k)$, so $4 \mid (y^2-x^2)$. Thus by definition, $y^2 \equiv x^2 \pmod{4}$. Therefore $R$ is symmetric. Finally, we will show that $R$ is transitive. Suppose $xRy$ and $yRz$ for $x,y,z \in \mathbb{Z}$. So $x^2-y^2 = 4k$ for some integer $k$ and $y^2-z^2 = 4l$ for some integer $l$. Adding these two equations, we obtain $x^2-z^2 = 4(k+l)$, so $4 \mid (x^2-z^2)$. Hence $x^2 \equiv z^2 \pmod{4}$. Therefore $R$ is transitive. The past three paragraphs have shown that $R$ is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. ::: :::/answer ## 11.2 Equivalence Relations {#sec-11-2} :::exercise[11.2.2]{#ex-11-2-2} Let $A = \set{a,b,c,d,e}$. Suppose $R$ is an equivalence relation on $A$. Suppose $R$ has two equivalence classes. Also $aRd$, $bRc$ and $eRd$. Write out $R$ as a set. ::: :::answer Since $aRd$ and $eRd$, the elements $a$, $d$ and $e$ lie in one equivalence class, and since $bRc$, the elements $b$ and $c$ lie in another. These are the two classes: $[a] = [d] = [e] = \set{a,d,e}$ and $[b] = [c] = \set{b,c}$. Each class $X$ contributes the pairs $X \times X$ to $R$, so $$ \begin{align*} R = \{ & (a,a),(d,d),(e,e),(a,d),(d,a),(a,e),(e,a),(d,e),(e,d),\\ & (b,b),(c,c),(b,c),(c,b) \}. \end{align*} $$ ::: :::exercise[11.2.4]{#ex-11-2-4} Let $A = \set{a,b,c,d,e}$. Suppose $R$ is an equivalence relation on $A$. Suppose also that $aRd$ and $bRc$, $eRa$ and $cRe$. How many equivalence classes does $R$ have? ::: :::answer $R$ has one equivalence class. Since $aRd$ and $eRa$, the elements $a$, $d$ and $e$ lie in one class, and since $bRc$ and $cRe$, the elements $b$, $c$ and $e$ lie in one class. As $e$ belongs to both, all five elements share a single class: $[a] = [b] = [c] = [d] = [e] = A$. ::: :::exercise[11.2.6]{#ex-11-2-6} There are five different equivalence relations on the set $A = \set{a,b,c}$. Describe them all. Diagrams will suffice. ::: :::answer $R_1 = \set{(a,a),(b,b),(c,c)}$\ $R_2 = \set{(a,a),(b,b),(c,c),(a,b),(b,a)}$\ $R_3 = \set{(a,a),(b,b),(c,c),(a,c),(c,a)}$\ $R_4 = \set{(a,a),(b,b),(c,c),(b,c),(c,b)}$\ $R_5 = \set{(a,a),(b,b),(c,c),(a,b),(b,a),(a,c),(c,a),(b,c),(c,b)}$. ::: :::exercise[11.2.8]{#ex-11-2-8} Define a relation $R$ on $\mathbb{Z}$ as $xRy$ if and only if $x^2 + y^2$ is even. Prove $R$ is an equivalence relation. Describe its equivalence classes. ::: :::answer :::proof By definition, $xRy$ means $x^2 + y^2$ is even. We want to prove $R$ is an equivalence relation, that is, $R$ is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. First, we will show $R$ is reflexive. Take any $x \in \mathbb{Z}$. Then $x^2 + x^2 = 2(x^2)$ is even, so $xRx$. Thus $R$ is reflexive. Second, we will show $R$ is symmetric. Assume $xRy$ for some $x,y \in \mathbb{Z}$. Since $xRy$, we know $x^2+y^2$ is even, so $x^2+y^2 = 2k$ for some integer $k$. From the commutative property of addition $x^2+y^2 = y^2+x^2$. So $y^2+x^2 = 2k$. From this it follows that $y^2+x^2$ is even, so $yRx$. Thus $R$ is symmetric. Last, we will show $R$ is transitive. Assume that $xRy$ and $yRz$ for some $x,y,z \in \mathbb{Z}$. Since $xRy$ and $yRz$, it follows $x^2+y^2$ is even and $y^2+z^2$ is even, so $x^2+y^2 = 2m$ and $y^2+z^2 = 2n$ for some $m,n \in \mathbb{Z}$. Adding these equations we get, $$ \begin{align*} (x^2+y^2) + (y^2+z^2) &= 2m + 2n\\ x^2+y^2+y^2+z^2 &= 2m + 2n\\ x^2+2y^2+z^2 &= 2m + 2n\\ x^2+z^2 &= 2m + 2n - 2y^2\\ x^2+z^2 &= 2(m+n-y^2) \end{align*} $$ So $x^2+z^2$ is even, so $xRz$. Thus $R$ is transitive. The three paragraphs have shown that $R$ is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. Therefore $R$ is an equivalence relation. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[11.2.10]{#ex-11-2-10} Suppose $R$ and $S$ are two equivalence relations on a set $A$. Prove that $R \cap S$ is also an equivalence relation. (For an example of this, look at Figure 11.2. Observe that for the equivalence relations $R_2, R_3$ and $R_4$, we have $R_2 \cap R_3 = R_4$.) ::: :::answer :::proof Assume $R$ and $S$ are equivalence relations on set $A$. We want to prove $R \cap S$ is also an equivalence relation. First, we show $R \cap S$ is reflexive. Take any element $a$ in $A$. Since $R$ is an equivalence, then $aRa$. Since $S$ is an equivalence relation, then $aSa$. Thus $a$ is related to itself by both $R$ and $S$, Hence $a (R \cap S) a$. Therefore $R \cap S$ is reflexive. Second, we show $R \cap S$ is symmetric. Take any $a,b \in A$ such that $a(R \cap S)b$. This means that both $aRb$ and $aSb$. Since $R$ is symmetric $bRa$. Since $S$ is symmetric $bSa$. Thus $b$ is related to $a$ by both $R$ and $S$, hence $b (R \cap S) a$. Therefore $R \cap S$ is symmetric. Last, we show $R \cap S$ is transitive. Take any $a,b,c \in A$ and suppose $a(R \cap S)b$ and $b(R \cap S)c$. By definition of intersection it follows $aRb$, $bRc$ and $aSb$, $bSc$. Since $R$ is transitive $aRc$. Since $S$ is transitive $aSc$. Thus $a$ is related to $c$ by both $R$ and $S$, hence $a(R \cap S)c$. Therefore $R \cap S$ is transitive. We have shown that if $R$ and $S$ are equivalence relations on a set $A$, then the intersection of $R$ and $S$ is also an equivalence relation. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[11.2.12]{#ex-11-2-12} Prove or disprove: If $R$ and $S$ are two equivalence relations on a set $A$, then $R \cup S$ is also an equivalence relation on $A$. ::: :::answer :::disproof The statement is false. For a counterexample, let $A = \set{a,b,c}$ and take the equivalence relations $R_2$ and $R_4$ from @ex-11-2-6, $$ \begin{aligned} R &= \set{(a,a),(b,b),(c,c),(a,b),(b,a)} \qquad \text{and}\\ S &= \set{(a,a),(b,b),(c,c),(b,c),(c,b)}. \end{aligned} $$ Then, $$ R \cup S = \set{(a,a),(b,b),(c,c),(a,b),(b,a),(b,c),(c,b)}. $$ We have $(a,b) \in R \cup S$ and $(b,c) \in R \cup S$, but $(a,c) \notin R \cup S$. So $R \cup S$ is not transitive, and hence is not an equivalence relation. ::: :::/answer ## 11.3 Equivalence Classes and Partitions {#sec-11-3} :::exercise[11.3.2]{#ex-11-3-2} List all the partitions of the set $A = \set{a,b,c}$. Compare your answer to the answer to [Exercise 6 of Section 11.2](#ex-11-2-6). ::: :::answer $\set{\{a\},\{b\},\{c\}}$, $\set{\{a,b\},\{c\}}$, $\set{\{a\},\{b,c\}}$, $\set{\{a,c\},\{b\}}$, $\set{\{a,b,c\}}$. ::: :::exercise[11.3.4]{#ex-11-3-4} Suppose $P$ is a partition of a set $A$. Define a relation $R$ on $A$ by declaring $xRy$ if and only if $x,y \in X$ for some $X \in P$. Prove $R$ is an equivalence relation on $A$. Then prove that $P$ is the set of equivalence classes of $R$. ::: :::answer To prove the statement above we will use the following consequence: if $X, Y \in P$ and $X \cap Y \neq \emptyset$, then $X = Y$. :::proof First we show that $R$ is an equivalence relation on $A$. Take any $x \in A$. Since the union of the sets in $P$ is $A$, there is some $X \in P$ with $x \in X$. Then $x, x \in X$, so $xRx$. Therefore $R$ is reflexive. Suppose $xRy$. Then $x, y \in X$ for some $X \in P$. But then $y, x \in X$, so $yRx$. Therefore $R$ is symmetric. Suppose $xRy$ and $yRz$. Then $x, y \in X$ and $y, z \in Y$ for some $X, Y \in P$. Since $y \in X \cap Y$, we have $X \cap Y \neq \emptyset$, so $X = Y$. Thus $x, z \in X$, which means $xRz$. Therefore $R$ is transitive. These last three paragraph shows $R$ is an equivalence relation on $A$. ::: :::proof Next we show that $P$ is the set of equivalence classes of $R$. First we need to show that if $x \in A$ and $X \in P$ with $x \in X$, then $[x] = X$. Suppose $y \in [x]$, so $xRy$. Then $x, y \in Y$ for some $Y \in P$. Since $x \in X \cap Y$, we get $X = Y$, so $y \in X$. This shows $[x] \subseteq X$. Conversely, suppose $y \in X$. Then $x, y \in X$ with $X \in P$, so $xRy$, which means $y \in [x]$. This shows $X \subseteq [x]$. Hence $[x] = X$. Now we show that every equivalence class of $R$ belongs to $P$. Let $[x]$ be an equivalence class, where $x \in A$. As in the reflexive case, there is some $X \in P$ with $x \in X$, and by the claim $[x] = X \in P$. Finally, we show that every set in $P$ is an equivalence class of $R$. Let $X \in P$. Since $X$ is nonempty, we can choose some $x \in X$. By the claim, $X = [x]$, so $X$ is an equivalence class of $R$.
