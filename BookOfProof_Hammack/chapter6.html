{.kicker} Worked solutions to selected exercises from\ Richard Hammack, *Book of Proof*, edition 2.2 # Proof by Contradiction {#chapter-6} ## Exercises {#exercises} **Use the method of proof by contradiction to prove the following statements. (In each case, you should also think about how a direct or contrapositive proof would work. You will find in most cases that proof by contradiction is easier.)** :::exercise[6.2]{#ex-6-2} Suppose $n \in \mathbb{Z}$. If $n^2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd. ::: :::answer :::proof For the sake of contradiction, suppose $n^2$ is odd and $n$ is not odd. Then $n^2$ is odd and $n$ is even. Since $n$ is even, $n = 2c$ for some $c \in \mathbb{Z}$. Then $n^2 = (2c)^2 = 4c^2 = 2(2c^2)$, so $n^2$ is even. Thus $n^2$ is odd and $n^2$ is even, a contradiction. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[6.4]{#ex-6-4} Prove that $\sqrt{6}$ is irrational. ::: :::answer :::proof For the sake of contradiction, suppose $\sqrt{6}$ is not irrational. Then $\sqrt{6}$ is rational. Then $\sqrt{6} = \frac{a}{b}$ for some $a,b \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $b \neq 0$, where $\frac{a}{b}$ is fully reduced; in particular, $a$ and $b$ are not both even. Squaring both sides gives $6 = \frac{a^2}{b^2}$, thus $a^2 = 6b^2 = 2(3b^2)$. Then $a^2$ is even, which implies $a$ is even. Since $a$ and $b$ are not both even, it follows that $b$ is odd, and hence $b^2$ is odd. Since $a$ is even, $a = 2k$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. Substituting this value for $a$ in the equation we get $(2k)^2 = 2(3b^2)$, so $4k^2 = 2(3b^2)$, and dividing both sides by 2 gives $2k^2 = 3b^2$. Thus $3b^2$ is even. But $b^2$ is odd and 3 is odd, and the product of two odd numbers is odd, so $3b^2$ is odd. Thus $3b^2$ is both even and odd, a contradiction. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[6.6]{#ex-6-6} If $a,b \in \mathbb{Z}$, then $a^2-4b-2 \neq 0$. ::: :::answer :::proof For the sake of contradiction, suppose $a,b \in \mathbb{Z}$ but $a^2-4b-2 = 0$. Then we have $a^2 = 4b + 2 = 2(2b + 1)$, which means $a^2$ is even. Therefore $a$ is even, so $a = 2c$ for some $c \in \mathbb{Z}$. Substituting this value for $a$ back in the equation we get $$ \begin{align*} (2c)^2 - 4b - 2 & = 0\\ 4c^2 - 4b - 2 & = 0\\ 4(c^2-b) & = 2\\ 2(c^2-b) & = 1 \end{align*} $$ Since $c^2 - b \in \mathbb{Z}$, the last equation says that 1 is an even number, a contradiction. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[6.8]{#ex-6-8} Suppose $a,b,c \in \mathbb{Z}$. If $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$, then $a$ or $b$ is even. ::: :::answer :::proof For the sake of contradiction, suppose that $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$, and that $a$ and $b$ are odd. Then there exist integers $m$ and $n$ for which $a = 2m + 1$ and $b = 2n + 1$. Hence, $a^2 = (2m + 1)^2 = 4(m^2 + m) + 1$, so $a^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$. Similarly $b^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$. Since $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$, it follows that $c^2 \equiv 1 + 1 \equiv 2 \pmod{4}$. But every square is congruent to either 0 or 1 (mod 4), so $c^2 \not\equiv 2 \pmod{4}$, a contradiction. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[6.10]{#ex-6-10} There exist no integers $a$ and $b$ for which $21a + 30b = 1$. ::: :::answer :::proof For the sake of contradiction, assume there exist integers $a$ and $b$ for which $21a + 30b = 1$. Then $1 = 3(7a + 10b)$. Since $7a + 10b \in \mathbb{Z}$, this implies $3 \mid 1$, a contradiction. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[6.12]{#ex-6-12} For every positive $x \in \mathbb{Q}$, there is a positive $y \in \mathbb{Q}$ for which $y < x$. ::: :::answer :::proof For the sake of contradiction, assume there exists a positive $x \in \mathbb{Q}$ such that every positive $y \in \mathbb{Q}$ satisfies $y \geq x$. Consider $y = \frac{x}{2}$. Since $x$ is rational, $x = \frac{a}{b}$ for some $a,b \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $b \neq 0$, so $\frac{x}{2} = \frac{a}{2b}$ with $2b \neq 0$; hence $\frac{x}{2}$ is rational. Since $x > 0$, we have $\frac{x}{2} > 0$ and $\frac{x}{2} < x$. Thus $y$ is a positive rational number with $y < x$, a contradiction. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[6.14]{#ex-6-14} If $A$ and $B$ are sets, then $A \cap (B - A) = \emptyset$. ::: :::answer :::proof For the sake of contradiction, assume $A$ and $B$ are sets, but $A \cap (B - A) \neq \emptyset$. Then there exists an element $x \in A \cap (B - A)$. Therefore $x \in A$ and $x \in (B - A)$. But if $x \in (B - A)$ then $x \in B$ and $x \notin A$. Thus we have $x \in A$ and $x \notin A$, a contradiction. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[6.16]{#ex-6-16} If $a$ and $b$ are positive real numbers, then $a + b \geq 2\sqrt{ab}$. ::: :::answer :::proof For the sake of contradiction, assume $a,b \in \mathbb{R}^+$ but $a + b < 2\sqrt{ab}$. Since $a, b > 0$, both sides of the inequality are positive, so squaring preserves the inequality: $a^2+2ab+b^2 < 4ab$. Subtracting $4ab$ from both sides, we get $a^2 - 2ab + b^2 < 0$. By factoring the left side, we arrive at $(a-b)^2 < 0$. But the square of a real number is nonnegative, so $(a-b)^2 \geq 0$, a contradiction. ::: :::/answer :::exercise[6.18]{#ex-6-18} Suppose $a,b \in \mathbb{Z}$. If $4 \mid (a^2 + b^2)$, then $a$ and $b$ are not both odd. ::: :::answer :::proof For the sake of contradiction, assume $4 \mid (a^2 + b^2)$ and $a$ and $b$ are odd. Then there exist integers $m$ and $n$ for which $a = 2m + 1$ and $b = 2n + 1$. Hence $a^2 = (2m+1)^2 = 4(m^2+m)+1$, so $a^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$. Similarly $b^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$. From the definition of divisibility, we know $a^2 + b^2 = 4k$ for some integer $k$. So it follows that $4k \equiv 1 + 1 \equiv 2 \pmod{4}$. But $4k = 4k + 0$, so $4k \equiv 0 \pmod{4}$. Thus $4k$ is congruent to both 0 and 2 (mod 4), which is impossible since $4 \nmid 2$, a contradiction. ::: :::/answer
